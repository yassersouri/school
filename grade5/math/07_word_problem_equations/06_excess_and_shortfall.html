🎒 The "Buy More, Need More Money" Puzzle
Some problems describe someone shopping with a fixed amount of money. If they buy a smaller number of items, they have money left over (an excess). If they try to buy a few more items, they don't have enough (a shortfall). From just these two clues, we can find the price of one item — without ever being told the total money!
Worked Example — Pencils
Sam has some money. If he buys 3 pencils, he has $6 left over. If he buys 4 pencils, he is $2 short. What is the price of one pencil?
Buy 3 pencils
+$6 left
Buy 4 pencils
−$2 short
Think about the jump from 3 pencils to 4 pencils — that's just 1 extra pencil. Buying that 1 extra pencil used up the $6 that was left over, and then also needed $2 more. So that one extra pencil must cost:
1 extra pencil = $6 (leftover) + $2 (shortfall) = $8
Price of 1 pencil = $8
Check: Sam's money = 3×8+6 = 30. Also 4×8−2 = 30. Both match! ✓

🔍 Once You Know the Price, Find the Total
After finding the price, you can find how much money the person started with. Use either clue — they'll both give the same answer:

Total money = (smaller count × price) + excess
Total money = (bigger count × price) − shortfall
Continuing the Pencil Example
We found price = $8. Sam's total money:
Using 3 pencils: 3 × 8 + 6 = 24 + 6 = $30
Using 4 pencils: 4 × 8 − 2 = 32 − 2 = $30
Both give $30 — that confirms our price is correct!

    🔄 Variant A: Money Left Over BOTH Times
    Sometimes buying more items still leaves money over — just less of it. The 1 extra item "eats up" the difference between the two leftovers.

    Price = (bigger leftover − smaller leftover) ÷ (difference in count)
    Example — Both Leftover
    Buy 3 → $12 left over. Buy 4 → $6 left over.
    Price = (12 − 6) ÷ (4 − 3) = 6 ÷ 1 = $6
    Check: 3×6+12 = 30. 4×6+6 = 30. ✓
    🔄 Variant B: Short BOTH Times
    Sometimes even the smaller purchase is short of money — buying more makes the shortfall bigger. The 1 extra item costs the difference between the two shortfalls.

    Price = (bigger shortfall − smaller shortfall) ÷ (difference in count)
    Example — Both Short
    Buy 4 → $8 short. Buy 5 → $15 short.
    Price = (15 − 8) ÷ (5 − 4) = 7 ÷ 1 = $7
    Check: 4×7−8 = 20. 5×7−15 = 20. ✓
